as Cyber Incident — Playbook

A generalized incident response framework for triggering a parallel cyber investigation track when a critical, actively-exploited vulnerability is confirmed present in the bank's environment. Designed so the Vulnerability Management team owns detection, scanning, and remediation while Incident Response coordinates broader investigation and mitigation.

Teams: IR · VM · CTI · SOC · Network · AppDefense

Section 1

The Core Concept: Two Parallel Tracks

Traditional vulnerability management treats a critical CVE as a patching problem. For a bank, a critical vulnerability with known in-the-wild exploits and confirmed presence in your environment is also a cyber incident — because the attacker may have already used the window between disclosure and your patch.

The key insight is that patching and incident response are not the same activity and should not be owned by the same team simultaneously. Think of it like a hospital that discovers a contaminated blood supply: the clinical team focuses on identifying affected patients and administering treatment (VM = scanning, patching, re-scanning), while a separate investigation team determines whether patients were harmed and contains the damage (IR = compromise assessment, threat hunt, containment, evidence preservation).

These tracks run in parallel — they do not block each other. Patching does not wait for IR to finish. IR investigation does not wait for patching to complete.

When does this playbook activate? All three conditions must be met: (1) CVE is on CISA KEV or has confirmed active exploitation in threat intel feeds; (2) CVSS base score ≥ 9.0 or vendor severity = Critical; and (3) VM team has confirmed the bank has one or more exposed assets. If conditions 1 and 2 are met but exposure is unconfirmed, activate a watchlist posture while VM scans.
Section 2

Team Roles & Responsibilities

🔴 Incident Response (IR)

  • Incident commander for the IR track
  • Opens and owns the incident ticket
  • Coordinates all non-VM response teams
  • Drives compromise assessment workstreams
  • Leadership communication and escalation
  • Evidence preservation and timeline
  • Closure and lessons learned

🟢 Vulnerability Management (VM)

  • Receives and assesses the advisory
  • Runs Tenable scans for exposure
  • Determines exploitability in bank context
  • Briefs IR on exposed asset inventory
  • Coordinates and tracks patching
  • Runs verification scans post-patch
  • Communicates patch status to IR

🟣 Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI)

  • Monitors threat actor targeting of sector
  • Provides IOCs, TTPs, actor attribution
  • Searches dark web / FS-ISAC for bank exposure
  • Produces threat assessment per CVE
  • Feeds threat hunt hypotheses to SOC
  • Notifies IR of new exploitation evidence

🔵 Cyber Threat Response (SOC)

  • Monitors SIEM/EDR for exploitation IOCs
  • Executes threat hunts from CTI hypotheses
  • Investigates alerts on affected asset classes
  • Reviews authentication logs / sessions
  • Supports IR with log analysis and triage
  • Escalates confirmed indicators immediately

🩵 Cloud & Network Services (Net/FW)

  • Implements emergency network mitigations
  • Applies WAF rules, ACLs, firewall blocks
  • Restricts access to exposed services
  • Provides network telemetry to SOC/IR
  • Implements compensating controls
  • Supports isolation of compromised assets

🟡 Cloud & Application Defense (AppD)

  • Investigates specific appliances / platforms
  • Pulls and reviews appliance telemetry/logs
  • Validates configuration, hardening status
  • Implements application-layer compensating controls
  • Supports forensic analysis of affected hosts
  • Expertise on specific tech (Citrix, Palo Alto, etc.)

Section 4

Threat Context — Why Banks Are Primary Targets

Financial institutions are disproportionately targeted during critical CVE exploitation windows. Understanding why informs both the urgency of activation and the threat hunt hypotheses.

Attacker timeline vs. bank response window
The "exploit window" problem for financial institutions Based on current threat intelligence, vulnerabilities appear in the CISA KEV catalog an average of 42 days after public disclosure — but exploitation often begins within hours of PoC publication. Financial institutions face scanning within 24 hours, targeted exploitation within 48 hours. Ransomware affiliates, Initial Access Brokers, and nation-state actors all specifically prioritize financial sector targets. A valid session token from a NetScaler appliance is a commodity item on criminal markets within days of exploitation becoming available.

Section 6

IR Response Phases — Detailed Steps

Scope of this section These phases cover the IR team's responsibilities. VM patching activities (Tenable scanning, patch deployment, verification scans) run in parallel and are governed by the VM team's own process. IR coordinates with VM but does not own or block patching.
Phase 0 Activation — trigger criteria and incident opening ⏱ T+0 to T+2 hours after VM confirms exposure ▼
0.1
Verify activation criteria are met
IRVM
All three must be true to activate this playbook:
① CVE is on CISA KEV catalog or has confirmed active exploitation per vendor advisory / CTI feeds
② CVSS base score ≥ 9.0 or vendor severity = Critical
③ VM team has confirmed ≥1 bank asset is exposed (Tenable scan positive or asset inventory match)

If ① and ② are true but ③ is pending: activate watchlist posture — CTI monitoring, SOC alert tuning, no full IR track yet.
⚠ Library-level CVE exception (e.g. Log4Shell class): For CVEs in embedded libraries or frameworks (Java, Python packages, JavaScript runtimes), Tenable infrastructure scans may return false negatives because the vulnerability lives inside an application's transitive dependency — invisible to a network or host scan. If the CVE affects a widely-embedded library, activate the IR track based on criteria ① and ② alone, then run a parallel SBOM-driven discovery track (see Phase 1.5) rather than waiting for Tenable to confirm exposure. Do not treat a negative Tenable result as definitive for library-level CVEs.
0.2
Open the cyber incident ticket
IR
Open a P1/Critical incident in the incident management system. Title convention: CRITVAL-[CVE-ID]-[YYYY-MM-DD] (e.g. CRITVAL-CVE-2026-3055-2026-05-14). Set severity based on exposure breadth: internet-facing = P1, internal-only = P2. Link the VM tracking ticket as a related record — they are sibling tickets, not parent/child.
0.3
Send initial leadership notification
IRMgmt
Notify CISO, relevant business line security officers, and enterprise risk within 2 hours of incident open. Initial notification should include: CVE ID, affected technology, number of exposed assets (from VM), current patch status, and whether active exploitation has been observed at the bank. Use the standard critical incident notification template.
0.4
Stand up the response team — initial bridge / war room
IRCTI SOCAppD Net/FW
Convene within 2 hours of activation. IR briefs the following using VM's exposure report: asset classes affected, network location (internet-facing vs. internal), business criticality, known exploit availability, and CTI context on active threat actors targeting this CVE. Assign workstream leads for each team and set cadence for updates (recommend every 4 hours while active).
Phase 1 Threat intelligence — understand who is exploiting this and how ⏱ T+0 to T+4 hours (runs continuously) ▼
1.1
Produce CVE threat actor and TTP assessment
CTI
Within 4 hours of activation, CTI delivers a written threat assessment covering: known threat actor groups exploiting this CVE; MITRE ATT&CK TTPs associated with exploitation; post-exploitation behavior observed in the wild (lateral movement, persistence, exfiltration patterns); industries and geographies being targeted; ransomware group involvement; and whether Initial Access Brokers are selling access obtained via this CVE.
For Citrix NetScaler example: Assess ransomware affiliates following Citrix Bleed playbook (session token theft → lateral movement → ransomware), state-sponsored actors, and IABs marketing session tokens from financial sector targets.
1.2
Search for bank-specific exposure on external intelligence sources
CTI
Search FS-ISAC sharing channels for sector-specific intelligence. Check dark web markets and forums for any mentions of the bank's domains, IP ranges, or brand. Check threat sharing platforms (MISP, ISACs, vendor intelligence portals) for IOCs specifically linked to the bank's ASN or IP space. Check Shodan / Censys for the bank's internet-facing instances of the affected technology (cross-reference with VM's asset inventory).
1.3
Develop threat hunt hypotheses and IOC list for SOC
CTISOC
Based on known exploitation TTPs, CTI produces a hunt hypothesis document for the SOC. This should include specific log sources to query, SIEM search queries for known IOCs, behavioral anomalies to hunt for (e.g. unusual session token usage, abnormal authentication patterns, unexpected lateral movement from VPN gateway IPs), and EDR/NDR detection signatures. 
-- Example hunt hypothesis: Citrix session token theft (CVE-2026-3055)
-- Hypothesis: attacker obtained valid session token via memory leak,
-- replayed it from an unusual source IP to authenticate as a legitimate user
-- Hunt: Citrix auth sessions with source IP mismatch vs. user's historical IPs
SELECT username, src_ip, session_id, auth_time
FROM citrix_auth_logs
WHERE auth_time >= '<ADVISORY_DATE>'
  AND src_ip NOT IN (
    SELECT DISTINCT src_ip FROM citrix_auth_logs
    WHERE auth_time < '<ADVISORY_DATE>' AND username = username
  )
1.4
Monitor CTI continuously — update IR on new exploitation evidence
CTI
CTI provides daily updates to IR on: new exploitation PoCs published, new actor groups observed exploiting the CVE, any bank-specific intelligence surfaced, and changes in targeting patterns. If bank-specific evidence of targeting or compromise is identified, CTI escalates to IR immediately — do not wait for scheduled update.
1.5
SBOM-driven discovery — for library / embedded component CVEs (Log4Shell class)
VMAppDIR
When a CVE affects an embedded library (Apache Log4j2, Spring Framework, OpenSSL, etc.), infrastructure scanning alone is insufficient. The vulnerable component may exist as a JAR-within-JAR, a transitive Python dependency, or a statically linked binary — none of which are visible to Tenable's network fingerprinting. This creates a false-negative risk that is structurally different from appliance CVEs.
Parallel discovery tracks to run alongside Tenable:
Track A — SBOM query: If the bank maintains Software Bills of Materials for applications, query them for the affected component name and version range. SBOM coverage is often incomplete — treat absence from SBOM as unknown, not clean.
Track B — Build artifact scan: Run a library-aware scanner (e.g. Grype, Syft, OWASP Dependency-Check, or Tenable's SCA module if licensed) against application build artifacts, container images, and WAR/JAR archives in Artifact Registry and deployment pipelines. This surfaces transitive dependencies invisible to host scanning.
Track C — Application team self-declaration: IR sends a structured query to all application development teams: "Does your application include [library name] version [affected range] directly or as a dependency?" Allow 24 hours for response. Escalate non-responders to their engineering leads.
Track D — Network-based detection (secondary): For Log4Shell-class JNDI injection CVEs, the SOC can deploy detection rules looking for JNDI-related strings in HTTP request headers and URIs across WAF/NGFW logs — these signatures indicate active exploitation attempts regardless of whether the host is confirmed vulnerable. 
# Track B example — scan container images with Grype for Log4j exposure:
grype IMAGE_NAME:TAG --only-fixed | grep -i "log4j\|log4shell\|CVE-2021-44228"

# Scan a local JAR/WAR archive:
grype dir:/path/to/application/ | grep -i "log4j"

# OWASP Dependency-Check against Maven project:
dependency-check --project BankApp --scan ./target/ \
  --out ./dc-report/ --format HTML
Phase 2 Threat hunt & appliance investigation — are we already compromised? ⏱ T+2 to T+24 hours (continues until closed) ▼
This is the most critical phase for financial institutions The question is not just "are we patched?" but "were we exploited before patching?" A bank may patch within 24 hours and still have a compromised appliance with a persistent backdoor or stolen session tokens in active use. Patching removes the vulnerability; only investigation determines whether the window was used.
2.1
Execute SIEM/EDR threat hunt against exposed assets
SOCCTI
Using the CTI-provided hypotheses and IOC list, the SOC executes structured hunts across: SIEM (authentication events, network connections, process execution on affected hosts), EDR (process anomalies, file creation, persistence mechanisms on hosts behind the affected gateway), NDR/NGFW (unusual outbound connections, lateral movement signatures, C2 beaconing patterns), and email/DLP (credential exfiltration patterns).
Document all hunt results — both positive and negative findings. Negative findings with documented methodology are evidence that we looked and did not find compromise, which is important for closure decisions and any regulatory review.
2.2
Appliance-specific investigation — pull telemetry and logs
AppDIR
Cloud & Application Defense investigates the specific technology affected. The exact activities depend on the CVE type, but generally include:
For network gateway appliances (Citrix, Palo Alto, Ivanti, etc.): Review full authentication logs for the exposure window; identify all sessions established during the window; cross-reference session source IPs against threat intel; check for configuration changes not authorized via change management; review VPN session logs for unusual volume, duration, or off-hours access; pull memory forensics if available; check for persistence mechanisms or backdoor users.
For server/application CVEs (Exchange, Apache, etc.): Review web server access logs for exploit-pattern requests; check for webshells in web root; review process execution logs for unusual spawned processes; check scheduled tasks and startup items for persistence; review user/group changes. 
# Citrix NetScaler example — pull ns.log for exploitation indicators:
grep -E "(SAML|SAMLRequest|AssertionConsumerService|NSC_TASS)" /var/nslog/ns.log |
  grep -i "error\|overflow\|leak\|corrupt" > citrix_anomalies.txt

# Check for unexpected admin account creation:
grep "USERADD\|group change\|adduser\|passwd" /var/nslog/ns.log |
  awk -v date="<ADVISORY_DATE>" '$1 >= date'

# Check active sessions with anomalous source IPs:
nsconmsg -d current -s 100 -g pepol | grep -v "<known_internal_range>"
2.3
Review authentication and session logs for anomalous activity
SOCAppD
For vulnerabilities that enable session hijacking or credential theft (e.g. Citrix Bleed class): review all VPN/gateway sessions established after the CVE was publicly disclosed; identify sessions from unusual geographies, unusual IP reputation, or outside business hours; correlate session user identity with downstream system access (what did the session do?); look for privileged account access immediately following gateway authentication.
2.4
Preserve forensic evidence
IRAppD
Before patching removes evidence: preserve appliance logs, memory dumps where available, network flow data for the exposure window, and SIEM data for the affected asset scope. Set log retention holds. If active compromise is suspected, do not patch before imaging — coordinate with IR on forensic preservation requirements.
Note: for most critical CVEs where no active compromise is found, normal patching can proceed. Forensic preservation is mandatory only if investigation reveals positive indicators.
2.5
Escalation criteria — from vulnerability response to active breach response
IRSOC
If any of the following are confirmed, immediately escalate to a full active breach response (invoke the breach IR playbook in parallel with this one):
① C2 beaconing or data exfiltration confirmed from an affected asset
② Unauthorized credential usage confirmed from appliance session logs
③ Webshell or persistence mechanism found on affected host
④ Lateral movement originating from an affected gateway or server
⑤ CTI confirms bank-specific data or credentials on criminal markets
⑥ Ransomware precursor activity detected (staging, credential harvesting at scale)
2.6
Third-party / vendor exposure sub-track — activate when CVE affects a vendor-managed system
IRCTIMgmt
Why this matters: The MOVEit campaign (CVE-2023-34362) breached Deutsche Bank, ING, Postbank, and Comdirect not through bank-owned assets but through a single third-party managed file transfer vendor all four shared. The bank's Tenable scan showed no exposure — because the vulnerability was in the vendor's environment, not the bank's. For CVEs affecting widely-used third-party software (managed file transfer, payroll processors, HR systems, print management, cloud storage), a parallel vendor sub-track is mandatory alongside the primary IR track.
Step 1 — Identify vendors in scope: IR queries Vendor/Third-Party Risk Management for all vendors using the affected software. Do not rely solely on the VM asset inventory — it covers bank-managed assets only. Ask: which vendors process or store bank data using this technology?
Step 2 — Notify and require attestation: IR (with Legal) sends formal written notification to each in-scope vendor requesting: (a) confirmation of whether they run the affected software version; (b) if yes, their patch status and timeline; (c) confirmation that bank data was not accessed or exfiltrated during their exposure window; (d) their own incident investigation results within an agreed timeframe (recommend 48 hours).
Step 3 — Scope the data exposure: Work with business lines to identify what data transits or resides with each in-scope vendor. Classify by sensitivity: PII, financial account data, payment data, credentials, internal documents. This classification drives regulatory notification assessment — a vendor breach of PII-containing MFT data may trigger notification obligations independently of whether the bank's own systems were compromised.
Step 4 — Validate vendor response: Do not accept vendor self-attestation alone for high-risk vendors. Request forensic evidence (patch confirmation, scan results, investigation report). For critical data processors, escalate to contract-level audit rights if vendor response is inadequate or delayed.
Phase 3 Network mitigation — compensating controls while patching proceeds ⏱ T+2 to T+8 hours ▼
Compensating controls are not a substitute for patching Network-layer controls reduce exposure but do not eliminate it. They run in parallel with VM's patch cycle — not instead of it. IR coordinates with Network/FW to implement; VM continues patching.
3.1
Determine appropriate compensating controls per CVE type
IRNet/FWAppDCTI
The right control depends on the CVE type. IR coordinates a brief decision call with Network/FW, AppD, and CTI to determine what is technically feasible and what residual risk remains from each option. Document the decision and any business impact.

3.2
Implement emergency firewall / WAF / ACL rules
Net/FWIR
Implement approved compensating controls through standard emergency change process. Document all changes with CVE reference, change window, approver, and expected business impact. All changes are reversible — document rollback procedure.

  source-address [ known-trusted-CIDRs ]
  destination-address [ citrix-vip-addresses ]
  application [ ssl web-browsing ]
  action allow
  description "CRITVAL-CVE-2026-3055 emergency restriction <DATE>"

3.3
Invalidate sessions and force re-authentication if session hijacking is in scope
AppDNet/FW
For CVEs that enable session token theft (Citrix Bleed class, authentication bypass): terminate all active VPN/gateway sessions established before the compensating control was applied; force re-authentication for all users; if technically feasible, rotate session signing keys/secrets on the appliance; implement step-up MFA for any privileged access that traverses the affected gateway.
Phase 4 Ongoing monitoring, status reporting & leadership updates ⏱ Throughout incident lifecycle ▼
4.1
Maintain incident status dashboard — updated every 4 hours
IR
IR maintains a live status summary covering: total exposed assets (from VM); patched count vs. unpatched count; compensating controls in place; threat hunt status (in progress / complete / findings); CTI assessment status; and any escalation decisions made. This is the single source of truth for all stakeholders and is the basis for leadership updates.
4.2
Leadership update cadence
IRMgmt
T+0 (initial notification): CVE identified, exposure confirmed, IR track open, VM patching in progress
T+24h: Patch progress, threat hunt status, compensating controls in place, no/possible/confirmed compromise
T+72h: Comprehensive update — patching complete %, investigation findings, closure projection
Immediate escalation trigger: any confirmed indicator of active compromise (see 2.5 escalation criteria)
4.3
Regulatory notification assessment
IRMgmt
A critical vulnerability with confirmed bank exposure requires a legal and compliance review of notification obligations. For US banks: assess under GLBA, NY DFS Part 500 (72-hour notification threshold for cybersecurity events), OCC guidance, and the FFIEC cybersecurity incident notification final rule (36-hour notification for banking organizations for computer-security incidents that rise to the level of a notification incident). This assessment should occur at T+0 and be revisited if investigation findings change.
Phase 5 Closure criteria & lessons learned ⏱ After remediation + investigation complete ▼
5.1
Closure criteria — all must be met to close the IR track
IRVMSOC
① VM confirms 100% of exposed assets patched or formally risk-accepted with compensating controls
② SOC confirms threat hunt complete — all hypotheses tested, results documented
③ AppD confirms appliance investigation complete — no indicators of compromise found or active breach response is open and managing any confirmed findings
④ CTI confirms no bank-specific intelligence on dark web or FS-ISAC channels
⑤ Compensating controls removed or formally accepted as permanent
⑥ Leadership sign-off on closure
5.2
Post-incident review — within 5 business days of closure
IRVMCTI
PIR agenda: timeline from advisory receipt to closure; dwell time between exposure and detection of exposure; patch deployment velocity vs. exploit window; effectiveness of compensating controls; threat hunt coverage gaps; lessons for playbook improvement. Key metric: how many days between CVE KEV listing and 100% bank remediation? Target is ≤14 days for internet-facing assets.
Section 7

Technology-Specific Guidance — Top 6 CVE Families in Financial Services

Reference investigation, hunt, and mitigation notes for the six CVE families with the highest confirmed financial sector impact. Each section covers the specific team with domain expertise (AppD), the SOC hunt focus, and network mitigation. Use these as checklists alongside — not instead of — the vendor advisory for the specific CVE in scope.

Citrix NetScaler ADC / Gateway (Citrix Bleed class — session memory disclosure)
AppD investigation priorities: Pull /var/nslog/ns.log and /var/nslog/httprequest.log for the full exposure window. Review SAML authentication log entries for crafted payload indicators (malformed SAMLRequest, missing AssertionConsumerServiceURL). Identify all sessions established during the window. Cross-reference session tokens against downstream system access logs — what did each session do after it authenticated? Check for new or modified nsapimgr users, cron jobs, or shell scripts in /nsconfig/. Run integrity check against known-good NSconfig baseline.

SOC hunt focus: Authentication from unusual IPs for known users who authenticated via Citrix during the window. VPN sessions outside user's historical pattern (geography, hours, device). Privileged access to AD or internal systems immediately following Citrix auth. Lateral movement originating from the NetScaler internal interface IP range.

Network mitigation: Restrict access to trusted IP ranges; enable geofencing for gateway if not already active; terminate and invalidate all active ICA sessions; if SAML IDP mode is active and the CVE targets that path (e.g. CVE-2026-3055), temporarily disable SAML IDP or restrict to known IdP IPs.
Palo Alto PAN-OS (RCE / authentication bypass)
AppD investigation priorities: Export and review system.log, threat.log, and traffic.log for the exposure window. Check for GlobalProtect exploitation attempts. Look for unauthorized admin account creation or config changes outside change windows. Verify running config against known-good backup. Check for persistence mechanisms (malicious extensions, modified boot sequence).

SOC hunt focus: Unusual management plane access. New admin accounts not created via approved workflow. Config changes during non-change windows. Outbound connections from firewall management IP to unexpected destinations.

Network mitigation: Restrict management plane access to dedicated management network; disable Global Protect portal access if temporarily acceptable; apply vendor-provided temporary mitigations (interface disable, feature flags) per advisory.
Ivanti Connect Secure / Policy Secure (authentication bypass / RCE chain)
Critical: Ivanti Integrity Checker Tool (ICT) is not sufficient CISA confirmed in multiple incident response engagements that Ivanti's own ICT — the vendor-recommended forensic verification tool — failed to detect compromise on devices that were in fact compromised. A negative ICT result is not evidence of a clean system. For Ivanti CVEs with confirmed active exploitation, independent memory and disk forensics are required. CISA has recommended factory reset + config restore from a known-good backup rather than patch-in-place for actively exploited versions.
AppD investigation priorities: Review ICS diagnostic logs and admin audit logs at /data/var/log/log.events and /var/log/. Look for webshell deployment in /data/var/dlbin/, /home/, or the web root. Verify all VPN user sessions established during the exposure window — identify downstream systems accessed from each session. Check for SAML token forgery if SAML authentication is configured. Check for persistence via modified startup scripts, cron jobs, or LDAP plugin alterations. Run the ICT tool, but treat a negative result as unconfirmed — proceed with independent log and filesystem review regardless of ICT output.

SOC hunt focus: Post-exploitation tradecraft for Volt Typhoon / UNC5221 (nation-state) differs significantly from ransomware. Nation-state actors use living-off-the-land binaries, avoid dropping traditional malware, and conduct slow credential harvesting before lateral movement. Hunt for: slow, low-volume LDAP queries from VPN gateway IPs; credential access to AD at unusual hours; outbound connections to typosquatted domains or cloud storage providers not in the bank's allowlist; commands executed via web interfaces that bypass standard admin workflows.

Network mitigation: Restrict management interface access to dedicated out-of-band management network. Limit SSL VPN outbound internet connections to required services only (limits payload download and C2). If exploitation is confirmed or strongly suspected and a vendor patch is not yet available, CISA guidance recommends factory resetting the appliance and restoring configuration from a known-good backup taken before the vulnerability window — do not patch in place. Confirm with current vendor advisory.

Key CVEs in this family: CVE-2023-46805 + CVE-2024-21887 (auth bypass + cmd injection chain — no auth required when chained), CVE-2024-21893 (SSRF in SAML component), CVE-2025-22457 (actively exploited by China-nexus actors per Mandiant/Google Cloud).
Fortinet FortiOS / FortiGate SSL-VPN (out-of-bounds write / RCE)
AppD investigation priorities: Export and review /var/log/log (system events), FortiGate traffic log, and SSL-VPN log for the exposure window. If FortiAnalyzer is deployed, pull centralized logs from it — it retains more history and is harder for an attacker to tamper with than device-local logs. Check for unauthorized admin accounts in config system admin. Verify running config integrity against the last approved backup in FortiManager. Known persistence technique for FortiOS exploitation: attackers have modified the SSL-VPN filesystem (specifically /data/etc/ and /migadmin/) to survive firmware upgrades — check for unexpected files in these paths even after patching.

SOC hunt focus: Unusual management plane access (HTTPS to port 443/8443 on the management IP from non-admin source IPs). New admin accounts or privilege changes not created via approved change workflow. Config changes committed during non-change windows. Outbound connections from the firewall management interface to unexpected IPs or domains. Post-exploitation lateral movement patterns: FortiGate compromise frequently precedes credential harvesting of AD, then ransomware staging on internal hosts.

Network mitigation: Disable SSL-VPN if not business-critical, or restrict access to specific trusted IP ranges. Restrict management interface access to dedicated OOB management VLAN — do not expose management plane to the internet or to production networks. Apply vendor-provided temporary mitigations (e.g. SSL-VPN portal interface disable) per advisory while patching. Note: CVE-2024-21762 specifically requires disabling SSL-VPN as a temporary workaround, not a WAF rule — confirm per advisory.

Key CVEs in this family: CVE-2024-21762 (CVSS 9.6 — out-of-bounds write, unauthenticated RCE via SSL-VPN), CVE-2023-27997 (heap buffer overflow in SSL-VPN, CVSS 9.8), CVE-2022-42475 (heap-based overflow, exploited by state-sponsored actors pre-patch). All on CISA KEV.
Apache Log4j2 / Log4Shell (CVE-2021-44228) — embedded library class
Tenable scans alone are insufficient for this CVE class Log4Shell and similar library CVEs require SBOM-driven discovery (see Phase 1.5). A negative Tenable result does not rule out exposure — the vulnerable JAR may be nested inside an application WAR file or container layer not visible to a network scan.
AppD investigation priorities: Use library-aware scanners (Grype, Syft, OWASP Dependency-Check) against all container images, WAR/JAR archives, and build artifacts. Query application teams for self-declaration. Review web server and application logs for JNDI lookup strings in HTTP headers (User-Agent, X-Forwarded-For, Referer) — these are exploitation attempt indicators: ${jndi:ldap://, ${jndi:dns://, obfuscated variants using ${${lower:j}ndi:. Check for unexpected outbound DNS and LDAP connections from application hosts during the exposure window, which indicate successful JNDI callback.

SOC hunt focus: JNDI injection strings in WAF / application logs. Outbound LDAP (port 389, 636) or RMI (port 1099) connections from application servers to external IPs — these indicate successful exploitation (JNDI callback). DNS queries to unusual domains from application servers. Unexpected process spawns from Java runtime processes (e.g. java spawning curl, wget, bash, or PowerShell).

Network mitigation: Block outbound LDAP, LDAPS, RMI, and DNS-over-HTTPS from application server network segments where not required — this breaks the JNDI callback mechanism and disrupts active exploitation even before patching. Deploy WAF rules to reject requests containing JNDI lookup patterns.

Long-tail risk: Log4Shell remains on CISA KEV years after initial disclosure. Financial institutions with complex application portfolios may have undiscovered instances in legacy systems, vendor software, or rarely-deployed internal tools. Re-run discovery whenever new application systems are onboarded.
MOVEit Transfer / Managed File Transfer (CVE-2023-34362 and follow-on CVEs)
Primary risk is third-party / vendor exposure — not bank-owned assets The Cl0p/FIN11 MOVEit campaign breached multiple major European banks not through bank-owned MOVEit instances, but through third-party vendors (payroll processors, data analytics firms, legal service providers) that ran MOVEit on behalf of the bank. Activate the third-party sub-track (Phase 2.6) immediately for any MFT CVE — do not wait for Tenable to confirm bank asset exposure.
AppD investigation priorities (for bank-owned MFT): Review MOVEit Transfer web application logs (%ProgramFiles%\MOVEit Transfer\Logs\ on Windows) for POST requests to /api/v1/token or /guestaccess.aspx at unusual times. Check for unexpected webshell files in the MOVEit web root (human2.aspx and variants were deployed in the original campaign). Review all file transfer activity during the window — identify what data was accessed or downloaded. Check database for unexpected changes to system tables.

SOC hunt focus: Unusual SQL queries to MOVEit database from the application service account. Outbound data transfers significantly larger than baseline. Inbound connections to MOVEit from external IPs not in the expected user base. HTTP 200 responses to the affected endpoints from unexpected source IPs.

Third-party track (see Phase 2.6): Identify all vendors who process bank data using MOVEit or equivalent MFT software. Formal written notification to each vendor within 24 hours of CVE KEV listing. Scope data exposure by vendor — classify PII, financial data, payment data. This classification drives regulatory notification assessment independently of bank asset status.
